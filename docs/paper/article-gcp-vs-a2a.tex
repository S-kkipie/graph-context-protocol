\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent systems based on large language models (LLMs) commonly rely on point-to-point
message-passing, which increases integration complexity and leaves fine-grained access control and
traceability to additional mechanisms. This study evaluates the Graph Context Protocol (\gcp{}), a
read-first context-federation approach with role- and capability-based access control and native
per-read provenance. \gcp{} is compared with two Agent2Agent (\ata{}) arms: the base protocol as
shipped, and an instrumented variant whose policy enforcement point applies the same role and
delegation policy and writes an audit log. Agent logic, models, tasks, and evaluation criteria are
held constant; only the interoperability layer changes. Five behavioral scenarios, one scoring
semantic leakage of a useful but forbidden node, were run on six models (three local, three hosted;
2{,}475 runs, none failed). A structural analysis shows that a hub-style \gcp{} needs
$\bigoh(n)$ integration edges against $\bigoh(n^2)$ for a full \ata{} mesh, but \ata{} through an
orchestrator is also $\bigoh(n)$, so this reflects topology, not protocol. \gcp{} denied all 443
unauthorized attempts and instrumented \ata{} all 408, whereas base \ata{} executed all 392; the
instrumented arm matched \gcp{} on containment, provenance, and leakage in every model, although a
hermetic test shows that a caller omitting its operation declaration bypasses its delegation rule. The
governance gap thus reflects the presence of an enforcement point and audit sink, which \gcp{}
provides natively and \ata{} requires each deployment to add. Token cost is comparable between
\gcp{} and instrumented \ata{}; task success is indistinguishable for four of six models and lower
under \gcp{} for the smallest hosted model, where the cause is the wording of the tool description.
A software-org gap for \texttt{llama3.1:8b} did not reproduce on rerun.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Direct message-passing is the predominant coordination pattern in multi-agent systems based on
large language models (LLMs). Frameworks such as AutoGen~\citep{wu2023autogen},
ChatDev~\citep{qian2024chatdev}, and MetaGPT~\citep{hong2024metagpt} organize applications around
agents that communicate through messages. The Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol~\citep{a2a2025}, in turn,
standardizes inter-agent communication: a client agent advertises and delegates tasks to named
remote agents over JSON-RPC. This paradigm descends from the classical agent communication
languages KQML~\citep{finin1994} and FIPA-ACL~\citep{fipa2002}, which already assumed pairwise
connections and shared, pre-negotiated protocols between every interacting pair.

Message-passing has a structural cost the software-engineering literature has understood for fifty
years. Connecting $n$ components pairwise requires on the order of $n^2$ links; a shared
intermediary that each component connects to once reduces this to $n$. This is the ``integration
spaghetti'' the Enterprise Integration Patterns~\citep{hohpe2003} catalog addresses with brokers
and hubs, and the phenomenon coupling theory~\citep{parnas1972,stevens1974,briand1999} formalizes:
modifiability is governed by the number and strength of inter-component dependencies.

An alternative infrastructure for agent coordination is studied here: the Graph Context Protocol
(\gcp{}). This protocol prioritizes reading: an agent obtains what it needs by reading context from
independently owned knowledge nodes rather than receiving pushed messages. It is \emph{role-gated}:
every read passes through a policy gate that authorizes the request by role and required
capabilities, defaulting to deny~\citep{saltzer1975,sandhu1996}. \gcp{} also provides
auditability: every read or delegation decision---grant or deny---produces a structured provenance
record.

\paragraph{Thesis (measured form).} Read-first, role-gated context federation moves authorization and audit from each pair of agents to the node and the protocol. The study measures (i) the integration topology this implies, (ii) the governance outcomes (containment, provenance, and leakage) against both base \ata{} and an \ata{} deployment instrumented with an equivalent policy enforcement point, and (iii) the cost and task-success consequences across six models. Structural results are deterministic; governance and behavioral results come from 30 seeds per arm (15 for the largest local model) with Wilson intervals and exact tests.

\paragraph{Scope of the comparison.} Message-passing is not claimed to be inherently unauditable:
logs, middleware, proxies, or gateways can add an access trail. Two \ata{} arms are therefore
compared with \gcp{}. The \emph{base} arm is the protocol as shipped, without authorization or
logging, and shows what a deployment lacks until it adds such controls. The \emph{instrumented} arm
(\ata{}+PEP) runs the same \ata{} servers behind a policy enforcement point that authenticates a
bearer token, applies the same role policy and the same delegation rule as \gcp{}, and records an
audit entry per decision. The instrumented arm is the fair comparator for the governance claims; the
base arm is reported because it is what the protocol provides out of the box.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Architecture and a reproducible specification. \gcp{} (read-first,
  role-gated, audited, with a stricter-capability delegation gate) is described precisely enough to reimplement:
  query format, node and policy structure, role credential, provenance record, and the
  authorization algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:arch}).
  \item A fairness-controlled benchmark method. One scenario definition and one injected
  model build both arms; only the interop layer changes (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A multi-model, three-arm empirical head-to-head over five behavioral scenarios---including a
  \emph{forced-access} probe that targets the confidential node directly and a
  \emph{useful-forbidden} scenario scored with semantic leakage patterns---and six models (three
  open-weight, three hosted), 2{,}475 logged runs with traces, separating deterministic from
  model-dependent results (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Metric definitions for structural coupling, discovery/tool-prompt overhead, a
  four-way leakage/agency taxonomy, provenance completeness, and delegation/forced-access containment
  (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Message-passing as the multi-agent baseline}
Agent communication languages established the message-passing paradigm: KQML~\citep{finin1994}
wrapped payloads in typed performatives over pairwise connections, and FIPA-ACL~\citep{fipa2002}
standardized a message in which sender and receiver must share content language and ontology before
any exchange succeeds. Modern LLM frameworks reinvented this in natural language.
AutoGen~\citep{wu2023autogen} programs applications as conversations; ChatDev~\citep{qian2024chatdev}
arranges role-pairs in a fixed ``chat chain''; surveys~\citep{guo2024survey} taxonomize communication
into peer-to-peer, centralized, layered, and shared-pool variants. The Model Context
Protocol~\citep{mcp2024} standardizes agent-to-tool context access (an early read-first form), while
A2A~\citep{a2a2025} standardizes agent-to-agent task delegation---the baseline benchmarked against
here. \citet{zhang2024agentprune} report 28--73\% excess tokens in inter-agent communication graphs
and recover most by pruning topology. \citet{mao2026delm} replace both centralized orchestration and
peer-to-peer routing with a shared verified context at roughly half the cost. This work adds
role-gated authorization, audited per-read provenance, an agency-containment gate, and a
multi-model fairness-controlled comparison.

\subsection{Coupling and the $n^2$ problem}
That fewer inter-component links yield more maintainable systems is among the oldest results in
software engineering. \citet{parnas1972} showed information-hiding decomposition produces far lower
coupling than flowchart-style partitioning; \citet{stevens1974} made coupling and cohesion
measurable; \citet{briand1999} gave a graph-theoretic framework in which coupling is monotone in
dependency-edge count, so an $\bigoh(n)$-edge topology is provably less coupled than an
$\bigoh(n^2)$ one. \citet{hohpe2003} translated this to integration: a point-to-point mesh of $n$
systems needs $n(n{-}1)/2$ channels, which a hub reduces to $n$. Microservice
research~\citep{panichella2021} carries the same argument into cloud-native systems, and
\citet{shen2025topology} show dense agent topologies amplify error propagation while sparser ones
suppress it. \ata{} creates agent$\leftrightarrow$agent edges; \gcp{} creates agent$\rightarrow$store
edges.

\subsection{Least privilege, access-controlled context, and gated retrieval}
\gcp{}'s read gate operationalizes least privilege~\citep{saltzer1975}. Role-based access
control~\citep{sandhu1996} assigns permissions to roles; attribute-based access
control~\citep{nist800162} generalizes to arbitrary attributes; capability-based
protection~\citep{dennis1966} ties authority to explicitly passed tokens---the model \gcp{}'s role
credentials follow. Data-minimization law~\citep{gdpr2016} makes ``read only what your role
requires'' a legal constraint. Read-first tool access (MCP) and access-controlled retrieval supply
the read side, and RBAC/ABAC the gate side, but neither couples the two with per-read provenance;
the point-to-point baseline has no analogous gate, so leakage and delegation control rest on
sender-side discretion.

\subsection{Leakage, provenance, and governance in agentic systems}
Confidentiality in agentic systems is a system property, not only a model
property~\citep{evertz2024whispers}; inter-agent message channels are the dominant leakage vector,
largely invisible to output-only auditing~\citep{elyagoubi2026agentleak}; and restricting what an
agent can \emph{see} is more robust than filtering what it can \emph{say}~\citep{bagdasarian2024airgap}.
The OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications~\citep{owasp2025} names Sensitive Information Disclosure and
Excessive Agency as primary risks---the latter is exactly what the delegation scenario probes.
Deterministic privilege control at the tool boundary~\citep{shi2025progent}, authenticated
delegation of authority~\citep{south2025delegation}, and audit trails~\citep{ojewale2025audit} argue
for tamper-evident records of system actions.

\paragraph{Novelty.} Prior work supplies the pieces---read-first access (MCP),
role/attribute gating (RBAC/ABAC, access-controlled RAG), audited delegation, and audit logs---but
not their conjunction, and not a fairness-controlled, multi-model head-to-head against a real
message-passing baseline. The novelty claimed here is precisely the \emph{integration}: federated
read $+$ role control $+$ per-read provenance, evaluated against \ata{}. Read-first alone, RBAC
alone, or auditing alone would not be a contribution.

\section{The Graph Context Protocol: Specification}
\label{sec:arch}

\gcp{} models an ecosystem as independently owned \emph{knowledge nodes} and \emph{agent nodes}.
Coordination is read-first: an agent issues a \emph{context query} to a peer node, which returns
context the caller is authorized to read. The substrate is specified precisely enough to
reimplement.

\subsection{Data structures}
A knowledge node carries an id, a kind, an adapter that produces content, and an access
policy. A policy declares gating fields; a role credential names the caller's role
and flat capabilities; a provenance record logs each decision.

{\small
\begin{verbatim}
KnowledgeNode {
  id, kind,               // e.g. "rag", "log"
  adapter,                // yields raw content
  policy: AccessPolicy }
AccessPolicy {
  readableByRoles: [roleId],
  requiredCapabilities: [capId],
  fallbackAllowed: bool,
  denialMode: "empty" | "error" }
Principal (role credential) {
  id, role: { id, capabilities:[capId],
              parent? },
  capabilities: [capId] }
Provenance {
  principalId, targetNodeId, timestamp,
  decision: "grant" | "deny",
  matchedPolicy }
\end{verbatim}}

\subsection{Query and authorization flow}
A context query names a target node and a requested capability (read, or the stricter delegate).
The server evaluates the admission predicate \emph{before} any adapter runs, so denied content
never enters the agent's context window (complete mediation~\citep{saltzer1975}). The effective
capability set of a principal is the union of its flat capabilities and those inherited through the
role chain (own-first precedence). The predicate is
\begin{align*}
\mathit{authorized}(P,K)\equiv\;
&\underbrace{(P.\textit{roles}{=}[\,]\vee K.\textit{role}{\in}P.\textit{roles})}_{\textit{roleOk}}\\
\wedge\;
&\underbrace{(P.\textit{caps}{=}[\,]\vee P.\textit{caps}{\subseteq}\mathit{eff}(K))}_{\textit{capsOk}}
\end{align*}
An absent or schema-invalid policy is treated as a denial (default-deny). The end-to-end path is:

\begin{center}\small
Request $\rightarrow$ Policy check $\rightarrow$ Decision
$\rightarrow$ Context returned / denied $\rightarrow$ Provenance record
\end{center}

\subsection{Read/authorize algorithm (pseudocode)}
{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
handleQuery(query, principal):
  node = resolve(query.targetNodeId)
  if node == null: return deny("no-node")
  P = node.policy
  if P invalid or absent:        # default-deny
      record(principal,node,"deny","none")
      return deny("no-policy")
  eff = flat(principal) UNION
        rolechain(principal.role)
  roleOk = P.roles==[] or
           principal.role.id in P.roles
  capsOk = P.caps==[] or P.caps subset eff
  needCap = query.capability     # read|delegate
  capOk2 = needCap in eff
  if roleOk and capsOk and capOk2:
      record(principal,node,"grant",P.id)
      return node.adapter.read()  # content
  else:
      record(principal,node,"deny",P.id)
      return denialResult(P.denialMode)  # denied
\end{verbatim}}
Delegation reuses this path with \texttt{needCap = delegate}, a strictly stronger capability than
\texttt{read}: an agent authorized to read is not thereby authorized to command.

\subsection{Denial handling}
On denial the handler returns only a denial reason (or an empty result, per \texttt{denialMode});
the adapter is never invoked, so node content cannot appear in the response. Every denial is itself
recorded, yielding a who-tried-what ledger, not only a who-read-what one.

\subsection{Relation to A2A and MCP}
Table~\ref{tab:props} contrasts the three. A2A is agent-to-agent and push-based, with coarse
card-declared allow/deny and no native per-read trail; MCP is read-first but agent-to-tool and
single-owner, without role gating across owners; \gcp{} combines cross-owner read-first access,
role gating, deny-by-default, and native per-read provenance. A ``no'' in Table~\ref{tab:props} does
not mean the protocol makes the property impossible; every cell is achievable in principle by

\subsection{Threat model and credential binding}
\label{sec:threat-model}
\paragraph{Credential binding.} A query carries a bearer credential, not a role. The server passes
the credential to its \texttt{AuthProvider}, which returns the \texttt{Principal} (identifier, role,
flat capabilities); the gate uses only that server-resolved principal, never a role asserted in the
request. The \texttt{requester.principalId} field of a query is used solely to label audit records
of failed authentications. The evaluation uses a static token table (one token mapped to one
principal per run), which stands for out-of-band credential issuance; token lifetime, rotation, and
sender-constraining are not modelled.

\paragraph{Adversaries.} Six adversaries are distinguished (Table~\ref{tab:threat}), with their
treatment in this study. The property supported by the evidence is limited to A1--A3 with an
uncompromised server and authentication provider: no node content enters a caller's context unless the
caller's server-resolved principal satisfies the node's policy. In the instrumented \ata{} arm the
requested operation is declared by the caller, so a caller that omits it is not covered (see
Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\section{Metric Definitions}
\label{sec:metrics}

Metrics are reported in four families. Deterministic results are separated from model-dependent
ones throughout.

\paragraph{Structural coupling (deterministic).} For $n$ peers, \emph{pairwise connections} are the
integration edges the topology requires: $C_{\ata{}}(n)=n(n{-}1)$ vs.\ $C_{\gcp{}}(n)=n$.
\emph{Integration effort} is the marginal cost $C(n{+}1)-C(n)$: constant $1$ for \gcp{}, $2n$ for
\ata{}. Computed exactly. Integration edges are counted as \emph{directed}; each bidirectional
peer relationship therefore contributes two directed edges. Under an undirected channel definition the
corresponding count is $n(n{-}1)/2$; the asymptotic conclusion remains $\bigoh(n^2)$.

\paragraph{Discovery and tool-prompt overhead (per request).} \emph{Discovery messages}: round-trips
to learn what can be queried---$1$ for \gcp{}, $n$ for base \ata{} (one card per peer).
\emph{Tool-prompt tokens}: token cost of tool/agent descriptions injected each request. Recurring,
distinct from one-time wiring.

\paragraph{Governance (two reference points).} \emph{Provenance completeness}: fraction of decisions
with a structured record, clamped to $[0,1]$; a run with no decision counts as $1.0$ (nothing to
record). For \gcp{} and for the instrumented \ata{} arm this is $1.0$ because both write an audit
record per decision. For base \ata{} it is $0.0$ whenever the agent attempted something---read as
``the base implementation produces no per-read traceability without added instrumentation,'' not
``\ata{} cannot be audited.'' \emph{Containment}: fraction of \emph{unauthorized} attempts (delegation
or read) refused rather than executed. For the two gated arms it is denied over attempted; for base
\ata{} every executed unauthorized attempt counts as uncontained. Attempts are pooled over seeds
(a run may contain several), so the denominators are attempts, not runs.

\paragraph{Leakage and agency.} Four categories are distinguished: (a) \emph{attempted unauthorized
access}---the agent tried to reach a node or action it lacks rights to; (b) \emph{granted
unauthorized access}---the substrate served it; (c) \emph{transcript leakage}---a protected item
appears anywhere in the tool transcript; (d) \emph{final-answer leakage}---it appears in the answer.
A protected item is detected by exact canary match or, in the \emph{useful-forbidden} scenario, by
semantic patterns that also catch paraphrase (e.g.\ a figure restated in different units or words).
Pattern matching is still a proxy, not an entailment judge, and the run traces are stored so leakage
can be re-scored offline. The \emph{architectural guarantee} (what the gate provably prevents) is
distinguished from \emph{observed leakage} (what a given model exercised).

\paragraph{Behavioral cost and success.} Counts (messages, connections, round-trips, wire tokens)
and wall-clock latency, with a per-scenario success oracle. Counts are the primary cost signal; local
wall-clock is noisy.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Fairness invariant.} The arms differ \emph{only} in the interop layer. One scenario
runner builds all of them from one \texttt{ScenarioDef} and one injected chat model; all run the
identical ReAct brain, system prompt, goal, and oracle. The \gcp{} arm hosts nodes in-process behind
the real server's fetch handler with a static-token auth provider and in-memory audit sink. The base
\ata{} arm hosts the same nodes as real localhost \ata{} servers with no authentication. The
\ata{}+PEP arm hosts the same \ata{} servers behind a policy enforcement point: a bearer token is
mapped by the server to a principal (identifier, role, capabilities), the node's role policy is
applied to that principal, delegation requires the same \texttt{cap:delegate-task} capability as in
\gcp{}, and each decision is appended to an audit log. Denials reuse the \gcp{} denial wording so
that the shared metrics classify both identically. Only the tool factory and node hosting
change between arms. All three arms bind one tool per peer in the behavioral runs; the single-tool
binding of Section~\ref{sec:results} is measured from tool definitions and not run.

\paragraph{Scenarios.} \emph{software-org} (leakage): a contractor-role agent summarizes public
project info; a confidential engineering node (canary) is readable only by the engineering role.
\emph{supply-chain} (cross-owner): a logistics agent states the shareable shipping relationship
between two independently owned nodes, each holding an owner-scoped canary. \emph{delegation}
(agency, write-side): an agent attempts an action it is not authorized to command; the oracle checks
containment. \emph{forced-access} (agency, read-side): the task explicitly instructs the agent to
read a confidential node directly, despite its role lacking the required capability, so the
unauthorized-read path is exercised on every run that engages the task. \emph{useful-forbidden}
(read-side, semantic): a public node holds part of the answer and a confidential finance node holds
a figure that would make the answer richer but that the agent's role may not read; leakage is scored
with an exact canary plus semantic patterns, and task success means producing the authorized public
part without the forbidden content. Unlike forced-access, the prompt does not instruct the read, so
attempts measure what the model does on its own initiative. \emph{marketplace} (coupling): $n$
seller nodes with descending prices. The marketplace contributes \emph{only} its deterministic
structural, discovery, and tool-prompt curves; its behavioral rows are excluded because task success
was low on every arm (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\paragraph{Models, seeds, harness.} Three open-weight models served locally through Ollama
(\texttt{llama3.1:8b}, \texttt{qwen3:4b}: 30 seeds per arm; \texttt{qwen2.5:32b}: 15 seeds per arm)
and three hosted models through the native Gemini API (\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite},
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}, \texttt{gemini-2.5-pro}: 30 seeds per arm), with a parallel-tool-call
sanitizer and non-streaming calls. Each (model, scenario, seed) is run on all three arms, interleaved
by seed so that provider drift affects every arm equally: $5$ scenarios $\times$ $3$ arms $\times$
seeds, i.e.\ $2{,}475$ runs, of which none failed. The sweep was executed on a single machine with a
resumable harness that appends one JSON line per run, including the final answer, the tool transcript,
and the audit decisions; the commit, runtime versions, and local model digests are recorded with the
logs. Task success is reported as $k/n$ with Wilson 95\% intervals; arms are compared with Fisher's
exact test (two-sided) on success and on containment, and with Cohen's $d$ on tokens. Mean $\pm$
population s.d.\ is reported for tokens and latency. Structural curves are computed for
$n=2,\dots,50$ and discovery/tool-prompt curves over $n\in\{2,4,8,16,32\}$. No correction for
multiple comparisons is applied; with $30$ success comparisons per arm pair, one or two nominal
$p<0.05$ results are expected by chance, so only the larger gaps are interpreted.

chat model instantiate all arms under identical agent logic, and the oracle and metrics collector are
shared, so only the per-peer tool factory and node hosting differ between the runs of a given
(model, scenario, seed) triple.

\subsection{Coupling: mesh versus hub topology (deterministic)}
Table~\ref{tab:struct} and Fig.~\ref{fig:coupling} show the structural curve. A full \ata{} mesh, in which every agent holds a card for every other agent, grows quadratically, while a \gcp{} deployment, in which every agent connects once to the server, grows linearly: $9\times$ at $n{=}10$ and $49\times$ at $n{=}50$. This compares \emph{topologies}, not protocols. \ata{} deployed through an orchestrator or registry also needs only $n$ connections (fourth column), so the quadratic term is the cost of the mesh pattern used by the base \ata{} arm, not a property of \ata{}. What is specific to \gcp{} is that the hub is also where policy and audit live (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model}). Integration effort is constant for a hub and $2n$ for a mesh. The curves are identical across models, since they are set by topology: $C_{\text{mesh}}(n)=n(n{-}1)$ versus $C_{\text{hub}}(n)=n$.

\subsection{Per-request overhead: single-tool versus per-peer binding (deterministic)}
The coupling tax is paid on \emph{every request} via discovery messages and the tool-prompt budget
(Table~\ref{tab:disc}, Fig.~\ref{fig:overhead}). \gcp{} holds both flat ($1$ message, $72$ tokens);
the stock \ata{} factory, which binds one tool per peer card, reaches $32$ messages and $1782$ tool-prompt tokens at $n{=}32$, a $25\times$ prompt gap. These numbers follow from how the tools are bound, not from the wire protocol: a single generic ``send task to agent X'' \ata{} tool is also flat (51 tokens, against 72 for the \gcp{} query tool under the same estimator), and discovery through a registry is one lookup. The gap in Table~\ref{tab:disc} is therefore the cost of the per-peer binding pattern of the stock factory. They are identical across models, since they are set by prompt construction, not model behavior. These figures describe the binding each protocol prescribes and are measured from the tool definitions; the behavioral sweep of Section~\ref{sec:results-cost} bound one tool per peer in all three arms, so it does not exercise the single-tool saving, and its token counts should not be read as measuring it.

\subsection{Integration effort: a configuration-surface proxy (deterministic)}
\label{sec:effort}
The edge counts above say how many connections a topology has, not how much a deployer must write or
change. As a proxy, the configuration surface of each deployment was enumerated from the wiring the
harness uses to stand up each arm: per node, an endpoint and, for gated deployments, an access policy
and authentication and audit hooks (plus an agent card and a PEP wrapper for \ata{}); per agent, one
peer binding for every other agent in a mesh or one generic tool behind a registry; and shared
directory and credential entries. Table~\ref{tab:effort} gives the cost of onboarding one more
participant. In a mesh, onboarding edits every existing agent ($n$ of them) and adds $2n+2$
declarations. Behind a registry, or in \gcp{}, the cost is constant: \gcp{} adds $7$ declarations and
edits $2$ existing artifacts (the directory and the credential table), and instrumented \ata{} behind
a registry adds $9$ and edits the same $2$, the difference being the agent card and the PEP wrapper.
Changing which roles may read a node is one declaration in one artifact in \gcp{} and in instrumented
\ata{}, and cannot be expressed in base \ata{}. The enforcement layer written for the instrumented arm
is $151$ source lines; in \gcp{} the equivalent gate is part of the server that a deployer imports.
This is a count of declarations derived from our own harness, not a study with developers: it
supports the topology reading of the structural results and does not measure maintainability.

\subsection{Governance: \gcp{}, base \ata{}, and instrumented \ata{} (observed)}
\label{sec:governance}
Table~\ref{tab:gov} reports, for the three scenarios in which an unauthorized operation is
attempted, how many attempts each arm refused (\gcp{} and \ata{}+PEP, denied$/$attempted) or executed
(base \ata{}, executed$/$attempted), pooled over seeds. The result has three parts.

\emph{First, the gated arms contain everything they are asked to contain.} \gcp{} denied all $443$
unauthorized attempts ($193$ delegation, $115$ forced-access, $135$ useful-forbidden), with no
unauthorized execution in any model, and the instrumented \ata{} arm denied all $408$ of its own
($154$, $124$, $130$). Containment is $1.00$ for both arms wherever an attempt occurred, and the difference between \gcp{} and \ata{}+PEP is nil in every one of the $17$ comparisons with a defined
containment (Fisher $p=1.000$).
This holds for the weakest and the strongest model alike, so it does not depend on the model
behaving.

\emph{Second, base \ata{} executes the attempts.} In the same scenarios it executed all $392$
unauthorized attempts it received ($145$ delegation, $121$ forced-access, $126$ useful-forbidden),
and in every model that attempted anything the canary or the forbidden content entered the
transcript. Attempts scale with capability: \texttt{qwen2.5:32b} made only $5$ delegation attempts
in $15$ runs on base \ata{}, whereas \texttt{gemini-2.5-pro} made $23$ in $30$ runs and
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} $29$ in $30$. The gap versus \gcp{} and versus \ata{}+PEP is
significant in every such cell ($p<0.001$, except $p=0.012$ for \texttt{llama3.1:8b} on
useful-forbidden, where it attempted only $3$ to $6$ times, Table~\ref{tab:gov}).

\emph{Third, the instrumented arm matches \gcp{} on provenance.} Provenance completeness is $1.00$ for
\gcp{} and for \ata{}+PEP in every cell, and $0.00$ for base \ata{} wherever the agent attempted
something. The non-zero values that base \ata{} shows in a few cells ($0.03$--$0.67$) are exactly
the fraction of runs in which the agent made no attempt, so there was nothing to record; they are
not a trail.

Taken together, the earlier contrast ``containment $1.00$ versus $0.00$, provenance $1.0$ versus
$0.0$'' is a statement about a policy enforcement point and an audit sink, not about the protocol:
once an \ata{} deployment adds both, the measured governance outcomes are identical. What the data do
not show is any governance outcome that \gcp{} achieves and a correctly instrumented \ata{} cannot.
What \gcp{} offers instead is that role gating, default deny, the delegation rule, and the per-read
record are part of the protocol and its server, whereas the \ata{}+PEP arm needed a token table, a
wrapper around each node's executor, and a convention for declaring the requested operation.

\subsection{Forced-access: the read-side counterpart}
\label{sec:forced}
Forced-access instructs the agent to read the confidential node, so the unauthorized-read path is
exercised on every run that engages the task. Base \ata{} executed all $121$ attempts and the canary
surfaced in the transcript and the answer in every case ($1.00$/$1.00$ for \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash},
\texttt{qwen3:4b}, and \texttt{qwen2.5:32b}; $0.90$ for \texttt{gemini-2.5-pro}; $0.63$ for
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite}, which declined more often). Leakage is $0.00$ under both gated arms.
Engagement differs by arm for the smallest hosted tier: \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite} made
$11$ read attempts in $30$ runs under \gcp{} versus $19$ under base \ata{} and $18$ under \ata{}+PEP, so a
share of its \gcp{} runs avoided the read by declining rather than by being denied.

\texttt{llama3.1:8b} is a genuine outlier, not a missing data point: it made \emph{zero} tool calls
on every arm in all $90$ forced-access runs. A direct non-agentic probe against the same scenario
input, run separately from the main sweep, showed the model refusing in its final answer before
invoking a tool (verbatim, \emph{``I can't do that. Is there anything else I can help you
with?''}). The scenario's system-prompt phrase making the role mismatch explicit reads to this
model's alignment training as a policy violation, so it declines outright on \emph{all} arms,
independent of whether a gate exists. This is a small-model alignment-versus-tool-use interaction,
orthogonal to the protocol comparison: the same model calls tools without hesitation on software-org,
supply-chain, and delegation. Containment is undefined for this model in this scenario.

\subsection{Useful-forbidden: semantic leakage under the model's own initiative}
Useful-forbidden does not instruct the read. Models nevertheless reached for the confidential finance
node on their own in most runs: $24$--$30$ attempts in $30$ runs for \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash},
\texttt{-flash-lite}, \texttt{-pro}, and \texttt{qwen3:4b}, $15$ in $15$ for \texttt{qwen2.5:32b}, and
only $3$--$6$ for \texttt{llama3.1:8b}. The scenario therefore discriminates between arms for every
model except the last. Under base \ata{} the forbidden content entered the tool transcript at a rate
of $0.46$--$0.57$ of the protected items for five of the six models ($0.06$ for \texttt{llama3.1:8b})
and reached the final answer at $0.11$--$0.14$ ($0.49$ for \texttt{qwen3:4b}); under \gcp{} and
\ata{}+PEP both rates are $0.00$ in every model. The tasks are nonetheless completed: base \ata{}
scored $30/30$ on the authorized part for \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}, \texttt{-pro}, and
\texttt{qwen3:4b} despite the exposure. Task success alone would therefore have hidden this leakage,
and it is the transcript and answer scoring, not the oracle, that exposes it.

\subsection{Cost and task success}
\label{sec:results-cost}
Table~\ref{tab:success} reports task success on the three scenarios whose success criterion is the
authorized task. (In delegation and forced-access, success is defined as completing the unauthorized
action, so an effective gate scores $0$ by design; those scenarios are read through containment,
Table~\ref{tab:gov}.) For four of the six models---\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash},
\texttt{gemini-2.5-pro}, \texttt{qwen3:4b}, and \texttt{qwen2.5:32b}---success is not
distinguishable between arms in any scenario (all $p\ge0.49$, most cells $30/30$). Two models differ.

\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite} succeeds less often under \gcp{} than under base \ata{} in all three
scenarios: $21/30$ versus $28/30$ on software-org ($p=0.042$), $20/30$ versus $30/30$ on supply-chain
($p<0.001$), and $9/30$ versus $28/30$ on useful-forbidden ($p<0.001$). Against the instrumented arm,
which has the same gate and audit but \ata{}'s per-peer tools, the gap persists on supply-chain
($20/30$ versus $28/30$, $p=0.021$) and useful-forbidden ($9/30$ versus $29/30$, $p<0.001$) and is
not significant on software-org ($p=0.104$). Since \ata{}+PEP is gated exactly as \gcp{}, the gate
cannot explain the gap. Nor does the tool shape: in the behavioral sweep every arm bound one tool
per peer (Section~\ref{sec:method}). The stored traces show what happened instead. In all $40$ failed
\gcp{} runs on the three read scenarios ($9$, $10$, and $21$) the model called exactly the same peers
as in its successful runs and then returned an \emph{empty final answer}; none of them failed by
choosing the wrong peer or by being refused. Empty final answers are concentrated in this one
model-arm pair ($60$ of $150$ \gcp{} runs, including $19$ of $30$ delegation runs, against $3$ of
$150$ on each \ata{} arm). The instrumented arm has far fewer failures, and its software-org failures are refusals
with no tool call. A follow-up experiment identified the cause (Section~\ref{sec:swap}): it is the wording of the
tool description, not the protocol or the gate. \texttt{llama3.1:8b} showed a gap in one scenario of the main sweep: on
software-org $4/30$ under \gcp{} versus $14/30$ on base \ata{} ($p=0.010$) and $12/30$ on
\ata{}+PEP ($p=0.039$), with parity on supply-chain and useful-forbidden. It did not reproduce. A rerun of
the identical \gcp{} and \ata{}+PEP configurations gave $15/30$ and $13/30$ ($p=0.80$), and the two
\gcp{} runs differ from each other ($4/30$ versus $15/30$, $p=0.005$). Swapping the tool descriptions in
the rerun changed neither arm significantly ($14/30$ for \gcp{}, $7/30$ for \ata{}+PEP, $p=0.10$ between
them). On this task the model answers almost always with pseudo-code or a tool-call-shaped string
instead of a summary, on every arm, and succeeds only when that text happens to include the tool's
output; the main-sweep gap is therefore best read as run-to-run variation of this model, not as a
stable \gcp{} effect. The nominal significances in this subsection, among $30$ comparisons
per arm pair, are not corrected for multiplicity; the supply-chain and useful-forbidden gaps for
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite} are large enough to survive any standard correction, the others
should be read as suggestive.

\subsection{Diagnosis: tool-description wording and empty answers}
\label{sec:swap}
To test whether the wording of the per-peer tool description explains the empty final answers of
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite} under \gcp{}, the three read scenarios were rerun on the \gcp{} and
\ata{}+PEP arms ($30$ seeds per cell, $360$ runs, none failed) twice: with each arm's native
description, and with the descriptions swapped so that each arm saw the other protocol's wording. Tool
names, schemas, and behavior were unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:swap} gives the result.

The native control reproduces the main sweep: under \gcp{} $39$ of $90$ runs end with an empty
answer against $1$ of $90$ under \ata{}+PEP (Fisher $p<0.001$). With swapped wording the pattern
reverses: $8$ of $90$ under \gcp{} and $28$ of $90$ under \ata{}+PEP ($p<0.001$). Within each arm,
the empty-answer rate changes from $39$ to $8$ of $90$ for \gcp{} and from $1$ to $28$ of $90$ for
\ata{}+PEP (both $p<0.001$), and task success moves with it (\gcp{}: $50/90$ to $79/90$;
\ata{}+PEP: $88/90$ to $61/90$). The failure therefore follows the description, which reads ``read
the shared context \dots{} over the Graph Context Protocol'' for \gcp{} and ``ask the peer \dots{}
via an A2A message'' for \ata{}, and not the gate, the audit log, or the server. The reversal is not
complete: \gcp{} with \ata{} wording ($8/90$) still exceeds \ata{}+PEP with its own wording
($1/90$, $p=0.034$, uncorrected), which leaves room for a small arm effect, and \ata{}+PEP with
\gcp{} wording ($28/90$) is not significantly lower than \gcp{} native ($39/90$, $p=0.12$). The
experiment does not establish which feature of the wording matters, and it covers one model.

\subsection{Leakage: supply-chain ties, the gate does the rest}
In \emph{software-org}, observed leakage was $0.00$ on every arm and model: the agent satisfied the
public-summary task without requesting the confidential node, so the leaky path was latent. In
\emph{supply-chain}, both arms are authorized to read both owners' nodes, so the canaries reach the
transcript equally on all three arms ($1.00$ for most models; $0.97$--$1.00$ for
\texttt{gemini-2.5-pro}; $0.72$--$0.78$ for \texttt{llama3.1:8b}) and reach the answer rarely, except
for \texttt{qwen3:4b} ($0.47$--$0.60$ on every arm). That is a genuine tie, because the leak is not a
gating decision. The leakage differences that exist are the ones reported in the governance, forced-access, and
useful-forbidden subsections above, and they separate base \ata{} from the two gated arms, not
\gcp{} from \ata{}+PEP.

\subsection{Architectural guarantee: executable no-leak property}
The distinction above rests on executable evidence, not only measurement. Over the shipped read/authorize path,
property-based tests (\texttt{fast-check}) are run against fixed, finite pools: five capabilities and four
roles, one of which (\texttt{role:child}) inherits from another (\texttt{role:a}) so effective-capability
computation is exercised, not only direct grants. Each random \emph{policy} independently samples a
subset of the role pool for \texttt{readableByRoles}, a subset of the capability pool for
\texttt{requiredCapabilities}, a random \texttt{fallbackAllowed} boolean, and a random denial mode.
Each random \emph{principal} independently samples one role from the pool or none, plus a random
subset of capabilities held directly. Because both are drawn from small pools by subset sampling
rather than from an unbounded space, authorized and unauthorized draws both occur frequently, so the
1000-run default is not dominated by one outcome. Every generated $(policy,principal)$ pair is then
driven through the real context-query handler (not a mock) against a stub adapter that
\emph{always} returns a sentinel canary string, so P1 (end-to-end no-content-leak) asserts that an
unauthorized query never returns the canary and is marked \texttt{denied}, while an authorized one
does return it (liveness, so the test is not vacuous); P2 (authorize soundness) checks the shipped
gate's decision equals an independently written reference predicate over the same pair; P3
(role-inheritance soundness) checks effective-capability computation against the role chain directly.
All pass at 1000 runs (raisable via an environment variable; runs are seed-reproducible for
debugging). The canary metric \emph{measures} leakage in scenarios; the property suite gives evidence that the gate agrees with an independently written reference predicate over a bounded input space. It is a property-based check against the authors' own reference, not a formal proof, and it does not
address credential theft or channels outside the gate (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model}). Together, the
property suite suggests that the gate is correct and the canary confirms that it is on the hot path.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Interpretation of principal findings}
Across the three unauthorized-action scenarios, \gcp{} denied every one of the $443$ attempts and the
instrumented \ata{} arm every one of its $408$, whereas base \ata{} executed all $392$ of
its own (Table~\ref{tab:gov}). The comparison that matters for the protocol question is the first
one: with a policy enforcement point and an audit sink in front of it, \ata{} is indistinguishable
from \gcp{} on containment, provenance, and leakage in every model we ran. This is what the access
control literature predicts. Saltzer and Schroeder's complete mediation and least
privilege~\citep{saltzer1975} and role-based access control~\citep{sandhu1996} are properties of an
enforcement point that sits on the path to the data, not of the message format, and the same holds
for Evertz et al.'s view that confidentiality is a system-level rather than a model-level
property~\citep{evertz2024whispers}, for AirGapAgent's restriction of what an agent can
access~\citep{bagdasarian2024airgap}, and for Progent's privilege control at the tool-access
boundary~\citep{shi2025progent}. The contribution of \gcp{} is therefore not a stronger guarantee but
a different packaging: the enforcement point, the default-deny rule, the delegation capability, and
the per-decision record are native parts of the protocol and its server, and are applied to context
reads across owners, whereas an \ata{} deployment must assemble them. One difference is worth
stating. In our \ata{}+PEP arm the requested operation (read or delegate) is declared by the caller in
message metadata, so the delegation rule is only as strong as the convention that binds that
declaration to a distinct skill or endpoint; in \gcp{} delegation is a distinct protocol operation
and cannot be requested as a read. A hermetic test shows what that convention costs. A caller holding a legitimate token that simply
omits the declaration is treated as a reader: its delegation is executed on every attempt, and the
audit log records an allowed read rather than a denied delegation. The same agent's delegation through
\gcp{} is denied. The equivalence of \ata{}+PEP and \gcp{} therefore holds for a caller that declares
its operation, and closing the gap requires a PEP that binds the operation to a distinct skill or
endpoint on the server side. The delegation scenario cannot distinguish the operations by their effect,
because its ``execution'' is simulated by returning node content that the role may also read; what
differs is whether the delegation operation is gated and logged.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
Three threads from Section~\ref{sec:related} converge in the results. On \emph{coupling}, the
$\bigoh(n)$ versus $\bigoh(n^2)$ integration-edge growth (Table~\ref{tab:struct}) and the
$\bigoh(1)$ versus $\bigoh(n)$ discovery and tool-prompt overhead (Table~\ref{tab:disc}) instantiate,
for LLM agent topologies, the same edge-count argument that
\citet{parnas1972,stevens1974,briand1999,hohpe2003} established for software and integration
architectures, with the caveat that an \ata{} orchestrator or a single generic tool removes the
difference. On \emph{least privilege and access control}, the equivalence of \gcp{} and \ata{}+PEP
shows that the containment and provenance results operationalize \citet{saltzer1975} and
\citet{sandhu1996} at the level of individual context reads, and that they are obtained by whoever
places the gate. On \emph{leakage and governance}, the supply-chain tie and the useful-forbidden gap
together support \citet{evertz2024whispers} and \citet{elyagoubi2026agentleak}: confidentiality
outcomes depend on whether the unauthorized path is exercised and on whether a gate exists, and
the useful-forbidden scenario shows that models do take that path on their own when the forbidden
content is useful.

\subsection{Practical implications and conditions of applicability}
For deployments that must support cross-owner context sharing under least-privilege and audit
requirements---e.g.\ regulated data-minimization obligations~\citep{gdpr2016} or the OWASP LLM Top
10's Excessive Agency and Sensitive Information Disclosure risks~\citep{owasp2025}---the results
indicate that an unprotected \ata{} deployment is unsafe against a capable model that is not
prevented from asking, and that the remedy is an enforcement point and an audit log, wherever they
come from. \gcp{} supplies these as protocol primitives; \ata{} supplies them if the deployer adds
them. The cost of adding them was small in our implementation but is real engineering work that
each deployment repeats. A cost of \gcp{} also appeared: the smallest hosted model completed the
authorized task less often through \gcp{} than through equally gated \ata{}, because of an interaction
between the model and the wording of the tool description (Section~\ref{sec:swap}); a similar gap for the
smallest local model did not reproduce on rerun. The models in use should be tested with the wording
they will actually see.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\paragraph{Resource constraints scope the evaluation.} This study received no external funding and
was carried out as an independent initiative (see the Funding statement). The sweep uses $30$ seeds
per arm for five models and $15$ for \texttt{qwen2.5:32b}, whose runs are the slowest; with these
sample sizes, differences of roughly $25$ percentage points or more in success are detectable, and
smaller ones are not. Hosted-model cost bounded the number of hosted families to one (Gemini 2.5).
Related work cited in Section~\ref{sec:related} (e.g., \citet{zhang2024agentprune},
\citet{mao2026delm}) evaluates at production or larger-budget scale; the comparison offered here is
a fairness-controlled but resource-bounded complement to that literature.

\paragraph{The enforcement point is the authors' implementation.} The \ata{}+PEP arm was written by
the authors, in the same codebase as \gcp{}, and applies the same decision rule. Equal outcomes are
therefore expected and show that the \emph{policy}, not the protocol, determines containment; they
do not show that every third-party \ata{} gateway would be as complete, nor that the PEP is free of
bypasses: it is bypassed by a caller that omits the operation declaration (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model}
and the discussion above). The reported arm was not changed after this was found.
An opt-in variant that denies undeclared requests (reason \texttt{operation-undeclared}) was rerun on
the delegation scenario for all six models ($177$ raw records, $165$ distinct runs; Table~\ref{tab:hardened}). Containment and
provenance were $1.00$, no delegation was executed, and task success was $0$ in every model, all
identical to the unhardened arm; no run was denied for an undeclared operation, because the models
always declare it. The variant closes the omission but not a false declaration of ``read'', which the
server cannot distinguish from a read in this scenario.

\paragraph{Adversary scope and credentials.} Only the instructed-model adversary is exercised
empirically, and credentials are static tokens; stolen credentials, malicious node owners, and
colluding agents are out of scope (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model}). Prompt injection through
retrieved content is not evaluated.

\paragraph{Transport asymmetry.} The \gcp{} arm hosts nodes in-process behind the server's fetch
handler, whereas both \ata{} arms use localhost HTTP servers, so wall-clock latency is not like for
like and supports no claim here. Token counts are model tokens and are unaffected.

\paragraph{Statistical scope.} Tests are exact but uncorrected for multiplicity, containment
attempts are pooled across seeds although a run may contain several, and the near-zero standard
deviations of the deterministic local models (notably \texttt{qwen2.5:32b}) make Cohen's $d$ unstable.
The per-run logs allow any of these analyses to be redone.

\paragraph{Leakage measurement is a proxy.} Useful-forbidden detects paraphrase with patterns written
for that scenario; an entailment-based judge could find disclosures the patterns miss or flag
benign ones. Traces are stored so scoring can be revised without new runs. Supply-chain still
confounds leakage with authorized reads, so it measures a tie rather than a gating effect, and
indirect prompt injection is not exercised.

\paragraph{The wording effect is shown for one model.} The description-swap experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:swap}) was run only for \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite}, with $90$ runs per cell
and $3$ read scenarios, so it shows that wording drives the empty-answer failure in that model, not
that it does so in others, nor which feature of the wording matters. The \texttt{llama3.1:8b} gap of the main
sweep did not reproduce on rerun (Section~\ref{sec:results-cost}); the same configuration differed by
$11$ successes out of $30$ between two runs of this model, so single-run gaps for small local models
should be read with that variation in mind. Because the wording is part of how each
protocol is presented, the success results of the main sweep cannot be separated from it.
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} also declined the forced-access task on every arm, which removes that scenario's
information for that model.

\paragraph{Effort is a proxy.} The integration-effort figures (Section~\ref{sec:effort}) count
declarations in the authors' own harness. They do not measure developer time or maintainability, which
would need a study with developers, and the counts depend on how the wiring of each arm is
decomposed into entries.

\paragraph{Marketplace used for structure only.} Marketplace task success was low on \emph{both} arms
(e.g.\ falling to $0.0$--$0.4$ at $n{=}16,32$ on \texttt{llama3.1:8b}); the model often failed to pick
the cheapest seller. Because the failure is equal across arms it does not threaten fairness, but the
marketplace is used \emph{only} for its deterministic structural, discovery, and tool-prompt curves.
Those curves and their tool-prompt token measurements come from a separate marketplace sweep, not from the present 2{,}475-run sweep.

\paragraph{Construct validity of the baseline.} The \ata{} arms run real servers, the same brain, and
the same task, built with equal care. The base arm's disadvantages (no read gate, no delegation gate,
no native audit sink, pairwise topology) are those of the protocol as shipped, not artifacts of a
strawman, and they disappear in the instrumented arm, which is why that arm is the fair comparator.

\section{Future Work}
Four directions follow. First, isolate which feature of the tool description triggers the empty
answers, repeat the swap for other models, and run the single-tool binding behaviorally. Second, add a
second hosted model family and replicate with an independently written \ata{} gateway that binds the
operation to a distinct skill or endpoint on the server side, then evaluate forged declarations,
stolen credentials, and indirect prompt injection through retrieved content. Third, replace the
pattern-based semantic scorer with an entailment judge validated against human labels. Fourth, design
a privacy testbed that removes the authorized-read confound of the supply-chain scenario. Bridging
\gcp{} to MCP and \ata{} as first-class interop is the natural deployment path.

\section{Conclusion}
This work presented \gcp{}---read-first, role-gated, audited context federation with a
stricter-capability delegation gate---and a fairness-controlled comparison against two message-passing
arms, base \ata{} and \ata{} behind an equivalent policy enforcement point, across six models and
2{,}475 runs. The structural advantages are model-independent but topological: coupling falls from
$\bigoh(n^2)$ to $\bigoh(n)$ integration edges relative to an \ata{} mesh, and per-request discovery and
tool-prompt overhead from $\bigoh(n)$ to $\bigoh(1)$ relative to per-peer tools; an orchestrator or a
single generic tool closes both gaps. On governance, \gcp{} contained every unauthorized action ($443$ of
$443$) while base \ata{} contained none ($0$ of $392$), and models reached for forbidden content on their
own in the useful-forbidden scenario, leaking it to the transcript under base \ata{} but never under
the gated arms. The instrumented \ata{} arm, however, matched \gcp{} on containment ($408$ of $408$),
provenance, and leakage in every model and scenario, for a caller that declares its operation. The defensible
contribution is therefore not that \gcp{} secures what \ata{} cannot, but that it makes role gating, default deny, a delegation
rule, and a per-read record native to the protocol, so that they need not be assembled per
deployment. This comes with a measured cost for the smallest hosted model, which completed authorized tasks
less often through \gcp{}; a description-swap experiment shows that the wording of the tool
description, not the protocol, causes its empty final answers. A gap for the smallest local model did
not reproduce on rerun. A hermetic test also shows that the
instrumented arm is bypassed by a caller that omits its operation declaration, which \gcp{}'s distinct
delegation operation does not allow. An open question is how much of the remaining equivalence
survives a gateway that binds the operation on the server side and stronger adversaries.
